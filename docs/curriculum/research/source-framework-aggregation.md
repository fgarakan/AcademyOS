**Purpose:** Synthesis document to inform the Angles Academy OS curriculum engine. ## Section 1 — Source Framework Summary

### 1.1 USTA — American Development Model (ADM) / Net Generation Pathway

**Sources:** ustasocal.com Net Generation pages, ustanorcal.com junior pathway, Net Generation Pathway Regulations, USTA 10-and-Under Tennis materials.

**Developmental stages.** USTA organizes 5–18 year olds under the American Development Model with a four-color progression: Red (ages ~5–7), Orange (~7–9), Green (~9–11), and Yellow (12+). Within Red/Orange/Green, the system is intentionally non-linear past age 11 — players can move between Junior Team Tennis, Junior Circuit, and individual tournaments concurrently. The PlayTracker mechanism replaces the older stars-and-trophies model with cumulative point earning across competitive participation.

**Ball/court progressions.** Equipment scales with the child:
- **Red ball** — ~25% compression, foam or low-compression felt, very slow bounce, played on the shortest courts (~36 ft) so beginners can rally from day one.
- **Orange ball** — ~50% compression, played on a 60-ft court, ball is faster and bounces higher than Red but is still controllable for 7–9 year olds.
- **Green ball** — ~75% compression, played on a full 78-ft court, intermediate speed/bounce, the bridge to standard play.
- **Yellow ball** — full standard ball, full court, used once a player has demonstrated control on Green.

**Technical priorities.** USTA framing emphasizes grips, preparation, swing path, and ball placement at the introductory stages. Importantly, USTA explicitly de-emphasizes "racing to Yellow" — the philosophy is that maximum participation and match volume at the right ball color produces stronger long-term technique than premature progression.

**Tactical priorities.** Introduced gradually: rally sustainment first, then directional control (down the line, crosscourt), then point starting/ending (serve and return), then more advanced patterning. Match play formats begin as low-pressure non-elimination team challenges and build toward sanctioned tournaments.

**Movement/athletic priorities.** Foundational at Red — agility, balance, coordination, throwing/catching/striking competencies. Fundamentals before specialization.

**Competition/readiness priorities.** Introductory team-based formats (Team Challenges) lead into Junior Team Tennis (local team competition) and Junior Circuit (non-elimination round-robin), which feed into ranked Junior Tournaments organized in seven levels (Level 7 Intermediate up to Level 1 National Championships).

**Assessment ideas.** PlayTracker accumulates "play points" for participation and "win points" for match results, capping per event to prevent rank-hunting. Progression between ball colors is determined by USTA in consultation with the Local Play and Competition Committee — based on age, observed competencies, and accumulated participation.

**Progression principles.** Player-centered, age-appropriate, non-linear, participation-first. Strong commitment to long-term athletic development principles. The system explicitly accommodates players starting at any age and at any stage.

### 1.2 Tennis Canada — Whole Player Development Pathway (WPDP) / LTAD

**Sources:** tenniscanada.com Whole Player Development Pathway page, Tennis Canada news release on WPDP launch, tennisbc.org pathways, tennisontario.com LTAD PDF, tennispei.ca youth pathway.

**Developmental stages.** Tennis Canada's WPDP is an evolution of the older 8-stage Long-Term Athlete Development (LTAD) model into a 7-stage roadmap from first racquet to elite professional. The earlier LTAD stages — Active Start, FUNdamentals, Learning to Train, Training to Train, Training to Compete, Learning to Win, and Living to Win — remain conceptually visible. The WPDP also defines four distinct high-performance pathways depending on a player's skillset, maturation rate, setbacks, and the age tennis was identified as their primary sport.

**Ball/court progressions.** Tennis Canada uses the same red/orange/green progressive tennis approach with appropriate racquets and modified courts, framed as "play, develop, and compete with the appropriate racquet and balls, on the right size court, so players can be successful and develop the basics and an all-court game early on."

**Technical priorities.** Foundations of basic technique are built early but explicitly within a wider framework of "physical, psychological, tactical, and technical skills for future success." Strong language around perception skills as foundational to long-term tennis success. All-court game (not specialization) is emphasized through the early stages.

**Tactical priorities.** As players progress, they move toward "imposing their game style," "building solid patterns in keeping with their personality and skills," and developing a "competitive mindset." The philosophy is explicitly to develop tactical maturity over time rather than impose a system early.

**Movement/athletic priorities.** Drawing on the LTAD tradition, Tennis Canada heavily emphasizes the "10 S's" of training (stamina, strength, speed, skill, suppleness, plus structure, support, etc.) with the principle that each capacity has critical windows where training produces maximum benefit. Physical literacy — fundamental movement skills — is non-negotiable in early stages.

**Competition/readiness priorities.** WPDP includes specific training, competition, and development recommendations for each stage. Notably, Tennis Canada targets a 3:1 win-loss ratio in the annual competitive plan as a healthy indicator of appropriate level placement. There is also an articulated "5 Cs" coaching framework — Culture, Character, Confidence, Connection, Competence — as the values layer surrounding skill development.

**Assessment ideas.** Province-level testing protocols exist for both 6–10 and 11–14 age groups, covering physical capacities. Performance Standards (Bronze/Silver/Gold/Platinum) are tied to ranking and result milestones at age-specific bands (girls 13–21, boys 14–22), used for funding and program selection.

**Progression principles.** "Holistic," "long-term over short-term results," "well-rounded athlete." Critical messaging that some stages "make or break an athlete" — particularly the Training to Train stage — and that skipping it produces players who plateau later in their careers.

### 1.3 Tennis Australia — Hot Shots / Player Pathway

**Sources:** tennis.com.au Hot Shots pages, tennis.com.au Performance Pathways, victennisacademy.com.au Hot Shots program description, vidatennis.com.au Hot Shots stages, citycommunitytennis.com.au junior pathway.

**Developmental stages.** Tennis Australia's Hot Shots program organizes 3–10+ year olds across four colored stages — Blue (Launchpad, ages 3–5), Red (5–7), Orange (8–9), and Green (10–11) — before transitioning to Yellow (12+). Notably, each of the Red/Orange/Green stages is further subdivided into three internal levels: **Foundation, Intermediate, and Matchplay**. Yellow is treated as the post-Hot-Shots competitive stage.

**Ball/court progressions.** Mirrors the international standard:
- **Blue Launchpad** — fundamental motor skills, foam balls, smallest courts.
- **Red** — 25% compression ball, smallest court (3×8.23m or 6×10.97m), 21–23" racquets.
- **Orange** — 50% compression ball, modified court, two court sizes available depending on player needs.
- **Green** — 75% compression ball, full 10.97×23.78m court, 25–27" racquets.
- **Yellow** — full ball and court.

**Technical priorities.** At Blue, the focus is fundamental and perceptual-motor skills (walking, running, skipping, balancing, striking, catching, throwing) — i.e., athletic competence first, tennis-specific skills second. Red introduces tennis coordination but explicitly notes that "rallying with movement and control is not yet achieved." Orange is when rallying with movement begins to consolidate. Green is when control over depth, direction, and power becomes the focus.

**Tactical priorities.** Tactical complexity scales explicitly with stage:
- **Red** — basic rally games, personal-best framing, no real tactics.
- **Orange** — introduction of singles AND doubles tactics, offensive and defensive patterns, serving and returning skills, modified scoring formats.
- **Green** — more complex singles/doubles tactics, all-court play, refining shot tools.
- **Yellow** — strategic thinking, tactical awareness in full-court matches.

**Movement/athletic priorities.** Hot Shots' early stages are explicitly designed around fundamental movement skills (FMS) before tennis-specific skills — the philosophy that an athletic foundation precedes a tennis foundation. Coordination, balance, and tracking are named outcomes at Blue.

**Competition/readiness priorities.** The "Matchplay" sub-level inside each ball color is a meaningful design choice — it tells coaches and parents that competition exposure should happen at every stage, not just at Yellow. The broader Player Pathway has three streams (Team, Tournament, Program) with UTR-rating-based recommendations rather than rigid age gates, allowing a non-linear journey.

**Assessment ideas.** Progression is "based on readiness, not age" — coaches assess coordination, confidence, focus, and consistency. The internal Foundation → Intermediate → Matchplay progression within each color provides three explicit competency checkpoints per stage, which is a cleaner gating model than USTA's binary color transitions.

**Progression principles.** "Played at all stages through individual and team rally games, with a focus on personal bests." Strong emphasis on social-emotional outcomes (listening, communicating, leadership, managing emotions) alongside skill — the 5 Cs equivalent at TA is explicit in the marketing language even if not formally branded.

### 1.4 General Junior Development Best Practices

**Sources:** Long Island Tennis Magazine summary of Balyi's four-stage LTAD model, Human Kinetics tactical strategy literature, Athletes Untapped court coverage drills, TennisOne pattern materials, Medium article on tactical shifts (citing Cochet's bisector theory and 2024 Hawk-Eye validation study).

**Developmental stages.** Across the literature, the dominant macro-frame is Istvan Balyi's four-stage LTAD:
1. **FUNdamental** (ages 5–10) — fun, multi-sport, ABCs (agility, balance, coordination, speed), no periodization.
2. **Training to Train** (often "make or break" stage) — basic tennis skills, ancillary capacities (warm-up, hydration, taper), single-periodized year.
3. **Training to Compete** (mid-teens) — high-intensity tennis-specific training year-round, double-periodized.
4. **Training to Win** (16+) — triple-periodized, fully optimized for performance.

**Ball/court progressions.** Universal agreement on the red→orange→green→yellow progression with modified equipment and courts. The shared philosophy: success early breeds long-term retention; making it too hard too soon kills participation.

**Technical priorities.** The literature strongly emphasizes that technique should serve intention, not be imposed in isolation. Common phrasing: "intention → action → skill." Stroke production is taught in rally and game-based contexts rather than purely as pattern repetition.

**Tactical priorities.** Several recurring concepts dominate the strategy literature:
- **The Bisector Principle.** After every shot, recover to the midpoint of the opponent's available angles. Validated by 2024 Hawk-Eye research published in Scientific Reports confirming Henri Cochet's century-old "theory of angles." This is foundational positional logic.
- **Common patterns of play.** One-to-one, one-to-two, and two-to-one patterns, predominantly keeping the ball crosscourt.
- **Crosscourt as setup, not destination.** "Mindless crosscourt is just feeding a rally" — the pattern is to use crosscourt to set up the next ball.
- **Short ball recognition.** The ability to see and act on a midcourt or short ball is the gateway between defensive and offensive tennis.
- **Pattern recognition.** Reading shoulder/hip cues, opponent stance, and rally rhythm to anticipate. Trainable from age 8+.

**Movement/athletic priorities.** Court coverage as a meta-skill: anticipation + footwork + recovery. Split-step on opponent contact, recovery to bisector, return to ready position — universal recommendations.

**Competition/readiness priorities.** Match play is taught, not assumed. Several frameworks have a 25%-development / 75%-competition ratio at high-performance stages, inverted from earlier stages. Healthy ratios cited: 3:1 win-loss in Tennis Canada's plan; matches per development hour ratios scale with stage.

**Assessment ideas.** Across sources: structured intake (skill, movement, competition, learning behavior), reassessment cycles, progression based on objective signals not time-served.

**Progression principles.** Universal agreement on:
- Right ball, right court, right time.
- Athletic foundation before sport specialization.
- Game-based learning over closed-skill drilling.
- Match exposure at every stage.
- Long-term consistency over short-term intensity.

## Section 2 — Cross-Framework Patterns (Areas of Strong Consensus)

The three governing-body frameworks — USTA ADM, Tennis Canada WPDP, Tennis Australia Hot Shots — plus the broader literature, agree on the following:

**1. Color-coded progressive equipment is the standard.** Red → Orange → Green → Yellow is the universal scaffolding. The compression ratios (25/50/75/100%) and approximate court sizes (36ft / 60ft / 78ft / 78ft) are essentially identical across federations.

**2. Athletic foundation precedes tennis specialization.** Every framework names fundamental movement skills (agility, balance, coordination, throwing/catching/striking) as a prerequisite to technical tennis development. Multi-sport participation is encouraged through Red and into Orange.

**3. Progression is by readiness, not by age.** Although age guidelines exist, every framework explicitly says coaches should assess skill, confidence, consistency, and focus — and move players when they're ready. Premature promotion is named as a developmental risk.

**4. Match play is integrated at every stage.** No framework waits until a player is "ready" before introducing competition. Modified, low-pressure formats — team challenges, non-elimination round-robins, rally games — appear from Red onward.

**5. The "Training to Train" / Learning to Train stage is identified as critical.** All frameworks influenced by LTAD warn that this is the make-or-break window. Skipping or rushing it produces technical and physical ceilings later.

**6. All-court game development beats early specialization.** No framework recommends building a baseline-only or serve-and-volley-only player from young ages. Versatility is the goal through the early stages.

**7. Perception, anticipation, and decision-making are foundational, not advanced.** Tennis Canada in particular names "perception skills" as foundational to long-term success. The literature on the bisector principle, pattern recognition, and shoulder-read cues reinforces this.

**8. Tactical complexity scales with stage.** From rallying → directional control → simple patterns (crosscourt, approach-volley) → recognized patterns (one-to-one, two-to-one) → integrated game styles. The progression is consistent.

**9. Long-term participation is the explicit primary goal.** Every framework opens with language about "tennis for life," "lifelong love of the game," or "lifelong participation." Performance is downstream of retention.

**10. Coaching language matters.** Tennis Canada's 5 Cs (Culture, Character, Confidence, Connection, Competence) and Tennis Australia's social-emotional outcomes (listening, communicating, persistence, emotional management) signal a shared belief that coach communication shapes development outcomes.

## Section 3 — Differences in Emphasis Between Frameworks

While the core scaffolding is shared, each framework places different weight on different elements:

**USTA emphasizes participation volume and event variety.** The PlayTracker model, the three concurrent program streams (Team Challenge, Junior Team Tennis, Junior Circuit), and the seven-level tournament structure all reward time-on-court and match volume. USTA's frame is "play often, in the right environment, and progression follows."

**Tennis Canada emphasizes the holistic athlete and high-performance sequencing.** WPDP is overtly framed as a high-performance development roadmap — even though it accommodates recreational play, its language centers on Top-100 outcomes, four distinct elite pathways, and the 5 Cs as character formation. Tennis Canada has the most explicit physical testing protocols (separate batteries for 6–10 and 11–14) and the most articulated philosophy around critical training windows.

**Tennis Australia emphasizes sub-stage gating and match play at every level.** The Foundation → Intermediate → Matchplay tier structure inside each ball color is a unique design choice — it gives three checkpoints per color rather than one. TA also formally extends earlier than the others with its Blue Launchpad (3–5 year olds), making the funnel wider.

**Differences in tournament/competition philosophy.**
- USTA: extensive tournament infrastructure, ranking-based, funded by accumulated points.
- Tennis Canada: targets a 3:1 win-loss ratio as a healthy plan; tournament participation is more intentionally curated for development.
- Tennis Australia: matchplay is integrated within stages; the Player Pathway uses UTR rating to suggest opportunities rather than rigid ranking gates.

**Differences in physical assessment.**
- USTA: PlayTracker is participation-and-result oriented; physical testing exists but is less central to the published framework.
- Tennis Canada: explicit physical testing batteries with published standards; physical capacity development is named as central.
- Tennis Australia: less published structure around physical testing at junior level; readiness assessment leans toward observed competence.

**Differences in tactical articulation.**
- USTA: tactical guidance is light at the published-framework level; left to coach discretion.
- Tennis Canada: tactical maturation is named as an outcome of later stages but specific patterns are not heavily prescribed.
- Tennis Australia: tactical content is more explicit at Orange/Green (offensive/defensive patterns, serve-and-return tactics) than the others.

**Differences in stage count and age framing.**
- USTA: 4 colors, age-banded but flexible.
- Tennis Canada: 7 stages (WPDP), much wider age and developmental range, includes living-as-a-pro phase.
- Tennis Australia: 4 colors, but each subdivided into 3 sub-stages (effectively 12 levels) before Yellow.

## Section 4 — Angles Interpretation: How the Angles Philosophy Synthesizes and Improves on These Frameworks

The Angles philosophy — Learn / Train / Play, separate-but-connected Skill and Competition tracks, Swinget-based perception-action coupling, court-mapping decision trees, intention → action → skill, evidence-based level movement, positive coaching language — picks up real gaps in the existing frameworks and proposes meaningful improvements.

**1. Where existing frameworks describe stages, Angles describes a daily structure.** Learn / Train / Play is a session-level rhythm, not a career-level rhythm. None of the major frameworks specify what a single training day actually contains. Angles fills that gap: every session has a learning component (new concept introduction), a training component (rep volume in context), and a play component (match-like or pressure application). This is closer to how elite development actually happens day-to-day, and it directly supports the "intention → action → skill" loop.

**2. Where existing frameworks blend skill and competition, Angles separates them — and then connects them.** USTA, Tennis Canada, and Tennis Australia all assume that a player progresses on a single track that becomes more competitive over time. The reality is that skill development and competitive maturity are different dimensions, and they progress at different rates. A player can be technically strong but competitively underdeveloped, or vice versa. The Angles two-track model acknowledges this and lets each be assessed and progressed independently while still cross-feeding signals into each other.

**3. Where existing frameworks teach "patterns," Angles teaches court mapping.** The bisector principle and pattern recognition are validated science (Scientific Reports 2024), but most curricula treat them as advanced topics. Angles' court-mapping language — middle, crosscourt, short angle, line, transition, endgame — gives coaches and players a shared vocabulary from day one. The progression through these zones (middle → crosscourt → line, etc.) is itself a curriculum spine.

**4. Where existing frameworks emphasize technique, Angles emphasizes intention-driven technique.** "Intention → action → skill" is a more honest model of how skill actually develops. A player swings a certain way because they're trying to do something. The Angles philosophy asks "what are you trying to do?" before correcting the swing, which aligns with motor learning research on perception-action coupling.

**5. Swinget / preparation / perception-action coupling adds a foundational tool that no framework currently has.** The frameworks all acknowledge that perception is foundational, but none provide a specific tool for training it daily. The Swinget — used as a warm-up and timing trainer — operationalizes what Tennis Canada calls "perception skills are fundamental to long-term success." It is a concrete daily implementation of an abstract principle.

**6. Where existing frameworks gate by age or accumulated points, Angles gates by evidence.** Tennis Australia's Foundation → Intermediate → Matchplay sub-stages are the closest analogue, but they are still coach-judgment gated. Angles' commitment to evidence-based level movement — with clear next-level requirements visible to the player on a dashboard — removes ambiguity and creates intrinsic motivation. Players know exactly what they need to demonstrate.

**7. Where existing frameworks use generic feedback, Angles uses structured language.** Doing Well / Working On / Current Focus / Next Step is a specific, positive, action-oriented frame that maps cleanly to how parents, players, and coaches should each receive information. It also maps cleanly to AI-generated parent updates and coach session briefs — the same vocabulary structure works across all communications.

**8. Where existing frameworks add fitness as a separate concern, Angles plans for a Fitness Path.** The 10 S's of LTAD, the physical testing protocols, the strength/speed/stamina language — these are present but disconnected from the skill curriculum in most frameworks. A future Fitness Path that talks to Skill and Competition, sharing readiness and load data, would be a structural improvement no major federation currently delivers in an integrated platform.

**Net synthesis:** Angles takes the proven scaffolding (red/orange/green/yellow, athletic foundation first, evidence-based progression) and adds (a) daily session structure, (b) two-track parallelism, (c) shared court-mapping vocabulary, (d) intention-first technique development, (e) perception-action tools, (f) explicit gates, (g) consistent positive language, and (h) a future-ready architecture for fitness integration.

## Section 5 — Recommended Stage Structure for Angles Academy OS

The brief specifies 15 stages (Red 1/2/3, Orange 1/2/3, Green 1/2/3, Yellow 1/2/3, HP 1/2/3). Below is a synthesized articulation of what each stage represents, drawing on Tennis Australia's three-tier sub-stage logic (Foundation → Intermediate → Matchplay) but extending it consistently across all four ball colors plus a High Performance band.

**Naming convention for sub-stages within a color:**
- **.1 Foundation** — concept and fundamental coordination introduced; success rate emphasized over volume.
- **.2 Intermediate** — concept under control with movement and rally context; rep volume increases.
- **.3 Matchplay** — concept reliable under match-like pressure; integration into game play.

**Red 1 — Foundation.** First contact. Playing with the racquet. Tracking. Throwing/catching. Striking a stationary or fed ball. Court is a playground; rules are minimal.

**Red 2 — Intermediate.** Self-feed and rally with a coach. Beginning to land balls in target zones. Stationary serve over the net. Introduction of court geography (your side / their side; close to net / far from net).

**Red 3 — Matchplay.** Mini-rally games with peers. Modified scoring. First "matches" that are play-based. Beginning to keep score in simple formats.

**Orange 1 — Foundation.** Recognizable forehand and backhand groundstrokes with structure. Drop-hit and feed rallies. Volley basics. Modified serve with continental-grip-friendly motion.

**Orange 2 — Intermediate.** Rallying with movement begins. Direction emerges (crosscourt vs. down-the-line). First exposure to the middle / crosscourt zones in court mapping. Singles and doubles formats introduced.

**Orange 3 — Matchplay.** Sustained rallies under pressure. Recognizable patterns (one-to-one crosscourt). Serve-and-return as the start of a point. First non-elimination tournaments.

**Green 1 — Foundation.** Full court engagement. Depth and direction control. Recognizable spin (basic topspin). All four major shots usable: forehand, backhand, volley, serve. Court mapping vocabulary now active: middle, crosscourt, short angle, line.

**Green 2 — Intermediate.** Tactical patterns become stable: one-to-one, one-to-two, two-to-one. Crosscourt as setup, line as finish. Transition zone awareness — recognizing the short ball. Approach and volley as a unit.

**Green 3 — Matchplay.** Match play in full-court formats. Beginning to impose game style. First experience with structured competition (rounds, sets). Bisector recovery is a coached habit.

**Yellow 1 — Foundation.** Standard ball, full court. Stroke production refined. All technical fundamentals consolidated. Movement patterns reliable. Comfort under regular pace.

**Yellow 2 — Intermediate.** Tactical maturity emerging. Endgame awareness — recognizing when to finish. Defensive vs. offensive zone discrimination. First serious competitive exposure (sanctioned events at appropriate level).

**Yellow 3 — Matchplay.** Building a personal game style. Pattern-based play with intentional variation. Healthy 3:1 win-loss target in competitive plan. Beginning serious tactical preparation.

**High Performance 1 — Foundation.** Full physical preparation integrated. Year-round tennis-specific training. Tactical patterns under fatigue. Periodization in single-cycle form.

**High Performance 2 — Intermediate.** Double-periodized year. Tournament travel. Self-management of preparation. Tactical adjustments mid-match. Anti-pattern recognition (breaking own predictability).

**High Performance 3 — Matchplay.** Triple-periodized. Performance optimization. National/international competition. Full ownership of preparation, performance, and recovery. Margins tighten — small gains drive results.

This structure preserves the Tennis Australia Foundation/Intermediate/Matchplay logic, applies it consistently to every color, and adds an HP band that matches the LTAD Training-to-Compete and Training-to-Win continuum.

## Section 6 — Recommended Curriculum Domains Per Stage

Every stage from Red 1 through HP 3 should include explicit content across these eight domains:

**1. Technical.** Stroke and shot tools relevant to the stage. From "racquet face awareness" at Red 1 to "spin-pace mix calibration under fatigue" at HP 3.

**2. Tactical.** Court-mapping content (middle, crosscourt, short angle, line, transition, endgame), pattern introduction, decision-tree complexity. Scales from "where to send the ball" to "why and when."

**3. Movement / Athletic.** Fundamental movement skills at the early stages (ABCs), tennis-specific footwork at the middle stages, sport-specific power and speed work at HP. Bisector recovery is taught from Green 1 forward.

**4. Competition.** Match play formats, scoring exposure, pressure application. Begins as rally games at Red, progresses through round-robins, structured tournaments, and into sanctioned competition.

**5. Learning Behavior / Mentality.** Listening, persistence, self-management, emotional regulation, focus, growth mindset language. Maps to the Tennis Canada 5 Cs and Tennis Australia social-emotional outcomes.

**6. Fitness Support.** Initially light and physical-literacy oriented; progressively builds toward strength, speed, endurance, recovery, and injury prevention. Foreshadows the future Fitness Path that connects to Skill and Competition Tracks.

**7. Assessment / Evidence.** What gets measured at this stage. Could include technical execution rates in target zones, rally length, accuracy under feed vs. live ball, match win rate at appropriate level, bisector recovery frequency, etc. Evidence is collected continuously; reassessment is structured.

**8. Level-Up Gates.** The specific, observable, evidence-backed criteria a player must demonstrate to move from this stage to the next. Visible to the player. Owned by the system, confirmed by the coach. Examples: "rally 10 balls with a peer at consistent depth," "execute the crosscourt-line pattern in 3 of 5 rallies in matchplay," "win 60% of matches at current ball color over a defined window."

## Section 7 — Raw Idea Bank: Skills, Drills, Games, Assessments, and Gates by Stage

This is a starting catalog — not a finished curriculum. Each idea is summarized, not specified to the level of a written drill card.

### RED (1, 2, 3)

**Skills.** Tracking a slow ball, throwing underhand and overhand, catching with two hands then one, striking a stationary ball, drop-hit a moving ball, swing without ball (shadow), simple grip introduction.

**Drills.** Bounce-and-catch, target throws into hoops, "tennis tag," racquet balance walks, ball roll relays, mini-rally with hands first, drop-hit-rally with coach.

**Games.** "King/queen of the court" red ball variants, beat-the-coach rally counts, target zone bingo, partner rolling games, mini-team challenges.

**Assessments.** Catches in 30 seconds, rolls into target box (count out of 10), stationary strike accuracy, follows simple instruction sequence, sustained attention duration.

**Level-up gates (Red 1 → Red 2).** Basic catching/throwing competence; can hold a racquet and contact a stationary ball; engages with peers in partner activities.

**Level-up gates (Red 2 → Red 3).** Drop-hit rally with coach to a target zone; serves the ball over the modified net; knows score in a simple rally game.

**Level-up gates (Red 3 → Orange 1).** Sustained mini-rally with peer (3+ shots in a row); plays a full mini-match using modified scoring; recognizable forehand and backhand swing shape.

### ORANGE (1, 2, 3)

**Skills.** Recognizable forehand, recognizable backhand, basic volley, modified serve with continental-grip-friendly motion, split-step introduction, ready position.

**Drills.** Crosscourt rally targets (mini-court), figure-eight rally (forehand-to-forehand, backhand-to-backhand), "two-bounce hit" timing drill, alley rally for control, serve-into-target progression.

**Games.** Doubles with cooperation rules, "21" rally points, king-of-the-court orange variants, serve-receive-rally games, target-zone scoring matches.

**Assessments.** Crosscourt rally count, direction accuracy on fed balls (cross vs. line), serve-in percentage at modified court, points-played-and-finished count in mini-match.

**Level-up gates (Orange 1 → Orange 2).** Reliable forehand and backhand under feed; can rally in crosscourt direction with a peer; basic volley contact.

**Level-up gates (Orange 2 → Orange 3).** Sustained rally with movement (5+ shots); serve and return to start a point; recognizes middle vs. crosscourt zones.

**Level-up gates (Orange 3 → Green 1).** Wins matches at orange ball at appropriate competitive event; demonstrates one repeatable pattern (e.g., crosscourt forehand setup); independent in basic match etiquette and scoring.

### GREEN (1, 2, 3)

**Skills.** Topspin emergence on groundstrokes, slice introduction, all four major shots usable, full court coverage, recovery to ready position, approach and volley as a unit, return of serve as a structured shot.

**Drills.** Full-court crosscourt drill (depth target), one-up-one-back doubles, line-vs-cross discrimination drill, transition ball recognition (short-ball-and-finish), bisector recovery shadow drill, serve-plus-one pattern.

**Games.** Approach-and-volley competition, baseline point games with required pattern, "first-to-five" with restricted shot selection, doubles tactics ladder.

**Assessments.** Rally consistency at full-court depth, first-serve and second-serve in-rate, pattern execution count (e.g., crosscourt-line) in live play, match win rate at green ball events, observed bisector recovery frequency.

**Level-up gates (Green 1 → Green 2).** Reliable groundstrokes from full-court positions; basic spin control; approach-volley as a recognized unit.

**Level-up gates (Green 2 → Green 3).** Demonstrates one-to-one and two-to-one patterns in match play; recognizes short ball and acts on it; bisector recovery becomes default.

**Level-up gates (Green 3 → Yellow 1).** Wins matches at green ball; demonstrates a personal game style emerging; serve and return are reliable point-starters; tactical decisions are intentional, not reactive.

### YELLOW (1, 2, 3)

**Skills.** Standard ball under control, full-court spin variation, slice and topspin both deployed tactically, tactical serve placement, return positioning, transition game.

**Drills.** Full-court pattern drills (cross-cross-line, line-cross-line, etc.), serve-plus-two pattern execution, defensive recovery from wide ball, endgame finishing drills, second-serve aggression progression.

**Games.** Pattern-restricted match play (must execute specific pattern to score), first-strike training, defense-into-offense transition games, doubles tactical scenarios.

**Assessments.** Match win rate at yellow ball appropriate competition, first-serve percentage and points-won-on-first-serve, return-of-serve depth, pattern fidelity under match pressure, fitness markers (work capacity, recovery between points).

**Level-up gates (Yellow 1 → Yellow 2).** Comfortable rallying at standard pace; all technical fundamentals reliable; first competitive yellow-ball matches with positive ratio at appropriate level.

**Level-up gates (Yellow 2 → Yellow 3).** Identifiable game style; tactical maturity in pattern execution; serve and return are weapons or at least neutralizers; physical preparation matches competitive demands.

**Level-up gates (Yellow 3 → HP 1).** Demonstrated ability to win at competitive sanctioned events at appropriate level; psychologically and physically ready for year-round tennis-specific training; ownership of preparation.

### HIGH PERFORMANCE (1, 2, 3)

**Skills.** All technical fundamentals at competition intensity, weapon refinement, tactical pattern variation, anti-pattern (breaking own predictability), advanced serve+1, serve+2, return+1 sequences, defensive transition to offense.

**Drills.** Live-ball pattern drills under fatigue, pressure point simulations, match-play with tactical constraints, opponent-modeling drills (mimicking specific opponent profiles), recovery-and-counterpunch sequences.

**Games.** Closed-set tactical match play, simulated tournament rounds, pressure-point exercises (set point, break point), opponent-style sparring.

**Assessments.** Match results at appropriate competitive level (national, ITF, college), advanced metrics (first-strike point win rate, pattern fidelity, defensive recovery rate), physical capacity tests (sport-specific protocols at academy/federation standard), mental performance markers (composure under pressure, set comebacks, etc.).

**Level-up gates (HP 1 → HP 2).** Demonstrated competitive results at appropriate level; physical preparation matches single-period demands; tactical patterns reliable under fatigue.

**Level-up gates (HP 2 → HP 3).** Double-periodized year successfully managed; tournament travel autonomy; tactical adjustments executed mid-match; healthy 3:1 win-loss in appropriate competitive plan.

**Level-up gates (out of HP 3).** Living-as-a-pro readiness — fully periodized, fully self-managed, performance-on-demand, and competitive at the next macro-level (ITF, college, professional, depending on pathway).
